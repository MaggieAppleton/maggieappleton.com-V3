# Three visual directions to discuss

These are alternatives, not a proposed sequence of three different metaphors. The conventional [reference sheet](exploration.png) is a way to inspect the numbers before choosing an essay treatment.

The two moments worth developing are already clear from the draft: making the actual responsibilities visible, and showing how little mothers' cognitive responsibility differs with employment or money. The first benefits from the raw records; the second needs the published adjusted models.

## 1. Responsibility fingerprints

**What it shows + why:** Every respondent has a different combination of responsibilities. A compact pattern for each person reveals the breadth of mothers' daily load, the clustering of fathers' reported responsibilities, and the real exceptions within both groups. This is the strongest reason to have acquired the microdata.

**Form:** Interactive matrix or a field of tiny respondent glyphs. Animation preserves each person's pattern while regrouping the display, making the shift from individuals to a population legible.

**Composition:** Start close enough to read one anonymous record: 21 cells in seven groups of three. Each cell is one named task. Solid cells mean “mostly me”; distinct neutral marks mean partner, shared, someone else or not applicable. A visible key prevents pale cells reading as tasks that do not exist. Zoom out to two panels of many such records, mothers and fathers. Sort within each panel by the number of daily responsibilities; the dense region becomes visible without a headline telling readers what to see. On choosing “Daily” or “Episodic,” highlight the relevant columns without changing cell sizes or inventing frequencies. Selecting a person reveals that record's actual responses, not a synthetic representative family. Keep panel scales and sample sizes explicit: a raw count of marks must not imply a fair comparison when one group contains more respondents. A density view can normalise by group size; a full census view should disclose the unequal Ns.

I would start here. It offers something averages cannot, although the initial close-up and a restrained response key are necessary to make the texture readable.

## 2. Two accounts of the same task

**What it shows + why:** The asymmetry involves what people think they own and what they think is shared. Showing the complete response distribution reveals both, while keeping the responses grounded in recognisable domestic details.

**Form:** A pair of interactive response mosaics with task illustrations or short written labels. Motion changes the selected task and response category rather than pretending that work physically moves between partners.

**Composition:** Place one task prominently between two equal-sized response fields, labelled “Mothers' responses” and “Fathers' responses.” For remembering appointments, the mostly-me areas represent roughly 83% and 34%; the equally-shared areas represent 11% and 30%. The reader can isolate a category, and the corresponding marks remain visible in both fields. Moving among seven domain tabs changes the task prompt while preserving the layout and response colours. A final zoom-out shows the 21 task pairs in a compact field, with the 15 daily items grouped separately from the six episodic ones. Make the dots represent percentage units, not matched individuals. Use exact recomputed fractions for areas, with rounded labels; if using published percentages, handle rounding visibly instead of quietly stretching the data to 100.

This is the most immediately readable option for a short note. Its cost is that it shows one task at a time; it cannot reveal how multiple responsibilities accumulate in the same person's answers.

## 3. The household control panel

**What it shows + why:** The paper's strongest comparison is between work that responds to employment or resources and work that does not show a significant reduction. A domestic-machine control panel could connect that finding to the essay's appliance imagery.

**Form:** An interactive comparison with three gauges or apertures, one each for cognitive responsibilities, care hours and housework hours. The movement communicates the reported relative differences, with an obvious stationary reference.

**Composition:** Above the three gauges, choose one comparison: employed/not employed, salary at least US$100,000/below that threshold, or higher/not higher income than partner. Each gauge has its own reference of 100, explicitly an index rather than a common unit of work. For high-income mothers the point estimates become 95, 70 and 83. Cognitive responsibility shifts a little, but is marked “no significant difference”; the two physical-work measures shift much more. Give all three gauges the same relative scale so the contrast is honest. Show reference and comparison together, with a brief transition when switching comparisons; there is no continuous salary slider. A mothers/fathers switch can reveal the different pattern among fathers, preventing a universal claim that resources never matter. Label these as adjusted group comparisons, not a before/after experiment or prediction about the reader.

This is the best companion to the vintage appliance material. I would use it as an alternative visual language for the whole evidence section, or keep a simpler non-metaphorical version beside the fingerprints. The exact uncertainty intervals need full-precision model information before we draw them; published significance labels are sufficient for an early sketch.

## A useful boundary for the next design discussion

Choose whether the main experience should make readers **inspect the accumulation of responsibilities** (fingerprints), **recognise specific tasks and conflicting perceptions** (two accounts), or **feel how stubborn the load is across resources** (control panel). Those are distinct editorial jobs. My preference is fingerprints for the main evidence and a small, direct adjusted comparison for the follow-up.

Avoid a mother/father pie or Sankey that treats their separate reports as complementary shares. Avoid recurring notification animations whose rate appears to come from the survey: recurrence was not measured. An agent “off” switch could be an explicitly speculative next scene, but none of these studies provides numbers for how much an agent removes.
