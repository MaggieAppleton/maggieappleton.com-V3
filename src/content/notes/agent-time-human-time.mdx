Every morning there is a new model, a new harness, a new skill to learn, or a new security breach to deal with, and the hits just won't stop coming.

[I wake up, there is a new model meme]

A significant chunk of this stress and discomfort comes from living inside a historical moment moving at warp speed. But there is a core piece of it unrelated to the unrelenting pace of novel developments, and instead about what our current agentic systems know about us, the humans, supposedly at their helm, steering their work.

More specifically, what they _don't_ currently know about our schedules, limitations, and needs.

Human attention, energy, intelligence, and emotional states are not linear and constant. When I sit down to work each Monday morning, fresh from a night of deep sleep,<Footnote idName={1}>This is a lie because I have a toddler but in a theoretical world, I imagine I could sit down at a desk fresh from eight hours of solid rest. Maybe some of us really do get to do this. I don't remember anymore.</Footnote> high on my first cup of caffiene, unburdened by last week's emotional dread or bug-bashing frustration, I am at my peak. I can make better judgement calls and come up with more creative solutions than I'll be able to at 5pm.

Over the course of my working day, my focus and energy ebbs and flows. I need to take regular breaks to keep it: bathroom breaks, tea breaks, leg stretch breaks, snack breaks, late-afternoon-coworker-chat breaks, gym breaks. And after 8-10 hours of staring into the screen, I need to wrap up and shut it all down for the evening. I need to close my laptop, pick up my kid from nursery, talk to my partner, eat a proper meal, lounge on the sofa watching some highbrow trash like [The Traitors]() or [Bake Off](), and be present in the not-work parts of my life, very far away from whatever the agents are doing. Then I need to lie flat and be totally unconscious for 7-9 hours in order to do it all again the next day.

This is the kind of balance humans need in order to do quality work, day after day. We need rest and breaks and sleep and warm meals and long evenings hanging out with our families. This is the basic shape of a Good Human Life, not an inconvenience to route around or squeeze into fewer hours.

[research: - What does science say about sustained attention, cognitive fatigue, rest, and decision quality across a working day, especially after hours of human-agent collaboration? Agents know nothing about this schedule or these needs. They are always awake and ready to do whatever task we need them to, without breaks or snacks. Their performance does not degrade over time (unless you count context rot, but I'd argue that is distinctly different to running time). Every moment is the same to them. They don't have any associations or expectations of what they should be doing at “8am,” “lunchtime,” or “the middle of the night.” 

In short, **they live in a completely different temporal world.** One that we have failed to design around the needs of humans. Our timelines are simply misaligned.

## Aligning Agents to Human Time

So what would it take to fix this misalignment?

If we are sincere in our [collective]() [expressed desire]() to make the agentic future a better place for humans (and not just for capitalist profits), we will need to design the agents around human circadian rhythms and rich personal lives.

Agents certainly already “know” about our typical human schedules and daily needs in their weights, but it's not something that activates every time we talk to them. They are not checking the time and reasoning about our energy levels or upcoming calendar events before they respond. And doing so would be a very naïve and context-heavy way to go about it.

When I say we should design around it, I mean we should give agents and their harnesses an understanding of human presence and absence that controls which mode they work in.

This can boil down to some simple questions: Is the human here or are they not? How long has the human been here, without a break? Do we expect them to leave soon or return soon?  These are simple, binary gates that can route agents into different kinds of work and change what they show in the human's interface.

If the human is here, we should treat their attention as precious, because it is. Use that time for judgement: expressing desires and preferences, defining success, planning and problem-solving, thinking together, comparing prototypes, coordinating with other humans, considering trade-offs, and reviewing interfaces, artefacts, and insights. The agents should focus on helping the human make the important, consequential decisions that only they can make. Strategic decisions, complex decisions, high-risk decisions, and creative vision decisions.

- **Human on-time:** surface important questions and trade-offs; provide the information and tangible prototypes needed to decide; pace interaction around attention and energy.

The agents should have been preparing for this while the human was away: sourcing problems from customer support, security, and a fleet of always-on review agents, triaging problems, researching problems, considering possible solutions to those problems, and designing bespoke ways to present the problems to the human to give them all the context they need.

While the human is present, the agent should be in a tight loop with them: problem solving and rapid prototyping their way through the stacks of hard questions. The human also needs to spend some of their time reviewing completed agent work to make sure it aligns with what they intended. As well as communicating and collaborating with other humans.

As soon as the human steps away, either for a short break or to enjoy a relaxing evening, the agents should dramatically shift their approach. Now it is time to build. Now it is time to rigorously implement the work, test it, review it six ways to Sunday, merge it, test it again, and write clear, concise reports on what they did and how they did it. Running slower or more thorough processes is completely fine, even preferable.

It is also time to prepare for the human's return: do we have decisions ready for them to make? What can we proactively investigate? Do we have high-level reports for them to review? 

The human should return to useful artefacts and the next decisions, rather than spend their time watching implementation or prompt an empty chat back into motion.

## Proactive agent time management

If I can be so bold as to suggest we go beyond this simple binary of human-on / human-off time, I think we can make agents active partners in protecting our time and energy balance. They can notice if I've been at the computer for five hours straight, making difficult decisions, and suggest a break, or direct me towards lighter work.

They can drive towards useful stopping points and create natural lulls. If it's getting late into the evening, they can prompt me to braindump my working context and schedule it to come back to me in the morning. Or simply tell me that I've made enough decisions for today. My pile is empty. There is no more to do.

I can hear the 996 grind boys of SF rolling their eyes at this kind of paternalistic design. I have not drunk enough caffeinated kool-aid to understand the benefits of their lifestyle. I promise we will not enforce it law (although I am a citizen of a country that limits trading hours on Sunday to protect everyone's rest time, so just know we've not above doing it)
- Classifiers can tell when we're getting emotional or frustrated and change their tone, suggest another task, or guide us towards a break.
- Designing for our best thinking means accounting for several scales of human time: attention and energy across hours; physical and social rhythms across a 24-hour day; weekdays and weekends; commitments, deadlines, rest, and recovery across days, weeks, and months.


## A daily rhythm for human-agent work

Agent switches modes between them and begins to learn your rhythms. Can see your calendar; see meetings, flights, travel, deadlines that may deviate from the standard schedule.

- **Morning:** review overnight work with fresh attention; inspect artefacts, make decisions, solve hard problems, define success, and line up independent work.
- **Across the day:** alternate human judgement with breaks and independent agent work.
- **Evening:** reach a clear hand-off with context, constraints, references, and tests of success.
- **Overnight:** agents implement, investigate, and prepare a concise return package for morning.

## Human energy as interface context

- Agents should adjust interface, effort, skills, focus, models, and behaviour around the human's mood, energy, enthusiasm, location, and activity. Current interfaces rarely express these different modes.
- Humans can still decide what they want to work on, this is not a fully agent-directed interface. Give them routes into different tasks: the review screen, the chat to coworkers screen, the what's happening in my company screen, the decision making screen, hard decisions, easy decisions. Ask what kind of work feels possible now: hard thinking and consequential decisions; generative exploration and prototyping; reviewing prepared work; lighter administration; or setting up independent agent work.
- Session length, time of day, interaction pace, and chosen work could offer context, but the agent should infer carefully without pretending to diagnose someone's internal state.

## Global rhythms of inference demand

At a recent conference, I had a conversation with someone who manages inference demand within Microsoft Foundry. [What is foundry? how much of the world's compute do they manage?]

They of course said it's hard to manage demand during peak hours, generally when the U.S. West Coast wakes up and joins all of Europe and the rest of America in hammering the agent servers.

But they also have the inverse problem: there is _tons_ of available inference overnight and on weekends that no one is using. All these valuable chips lie around fallow while most of the agent-driving humans sleep. Of course there is some amount of demand “following the sun” with Asia and the Pacific online while America and Europe sleep. But they still have more capacity than they know what to do with during slower hours and on weekends.

Show overlapping local working-hour peaks rather than one global wave, with sleeping homes and small marks for deferred jobs in quieter windows. Use the shared restrained palette, with dusty blue night and cream daylight. Label this as a conceptual request/workload pattern, not measured power, spare capacity, or pricing.]

So they're looking for ways to incentivise off-peak usage. \[Claude recently ran a promotion offering more capacity on weekends to incentivise off-peak use – face check, get a screenshot]

This is a lot like load balancing the electricity grid: overnight energy is cheaper, when few people are using it, and prices peak in the early morning and evenings when everyone is home with the lights on and running their washing machine.

I'm sure you can see where I'm headed with this: this is a symbiotic problem. Human rest periods align with lower-demand periods, making nights and weekends useful for asynchronous runs. If we can shift the vast majority of token-expensive, intensive agent work to off peak times, we spread the load.

At the moment, the work runs immediately after the prompt. But you do not always need the work immediately. You might not even need it the next day. Let's say it's Thursday and you don't need a result within the next 96 hours, because you are taking a long weekend. That is a huge execution window to work with.

## Scheduling agent work around deadlines

- Work shouldn't always run immediately after a prompt. Let someone say they need a result within two days or a week, then schedule it during lower demand: a load-balancing problem.
- The electrical grid offers a parallel: electricity tends to be cheaper overnight or off-peak. Could inference be cheaper too? Perhaps a job could cost two or three times less if run at 2 a.m. on Sunday. This is a possibility to investigate, not an established price relationship.
- Product choices: **run now** for a fast response at the current price; **run tonight** to finish by morning using spare capacity where available; **run by a deadline** at any point before a chosen day or hour; **run when cheapest** within an acceptable window.
- Deadlines and cost preferences become part of the prompt and model routing, rather than external project-management details.

## Different models for different moments

- Fast, responsive models are more valuable while the human is present and deciding; cheaper, slower models make sense overnight when no immediate response is needed.
- Daytime collaboration supplies the problem, constraints, references, and success criteria so final implementation can happen independently.
- Systems could change model, effort, tool access, and review depth according to human presence, deadline, cost preference, reversibility or consequence, and whether the next step needs human judgement.

## Interface and product proposals

- Show whether the system thinks the human is in on-time or off-time, with an easy override. Let people set deadlines and cost preferences rather than only choose a model.
- Make hand-offs explicit: what the agent will do, avoid, and bring back.
- Prepare a morning return package: what changed; artefacts/interfaces to inspect first; decisions requiring judgement; surprises, uncertainties, and failed paths; a recommended next move.
- Offer attention-based modes without wellness metrics, mood scores, or gamification. A calm opening could ask whether to think hard, explore, review, do lighter administration, or set up independent work. Show fewer consequential questions in light-work mode, comparison tools in review mode, and prototypes in exploration mode; use warm, spare design and human language.
- Track active collaboration time to offer stopping points. Preserve open questions, reasoning, references, and unfinished work so resuming after sleep or a weekend feels natural.

# Poential architecture model:

(very agentic workflows coded)

- Scouts look for problems and opportunities:
  - Product manager
  - Product designer
  - QA tester
  - Security review
  - Engineering review
  - Doc and DX improver
  - Customer feedback and support triage
  - Bug hunter and automated logs
- Scouts ingest feedback from all kinds of sources, run triage and prioritise
- Scouts hand off work that can be fixed automatically to workers
- Scouts feed non-automatable decisions into the main orchestrator who prioritises issues and hands off to a team of decision designers
- Decision designers prep the decisions for humans: research, consider possible solutions, prep evidence for each solution, prep visualisations and examples

Humans main interface gives them two modes: decide and review
- Review completed work: summary of what automated work has been done (firehose)
- Make decisions: review bespoke interfaces designed to help them make decisions

## Things I don't have a place for

What I'm presenting is a more palatable alternative to the always-at-hand vision of agents that's currently emerging. Agents working in the cloud, directable from your phone. You can talk to them anytime, anywhere. While you're playing with your kid. While you're eating dinner. While you're having coffee with a friend. They never sleep. You can text them from bed. From the toilet. They will ping you as soon as they need you, anytime, anywhere.
