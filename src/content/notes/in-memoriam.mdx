The First World War wasn't part of my history curriculum at school. For whatever reason, the British international schools I attended took a depth-over-breadth approach by picking very narrow, specific historical events and then making us memorise every last leader, date, battle, city, and negotiation treaty within them.

I know an exceptional amount about the Holocaust, the Arab-Israeli conflict, the Partition of India and Pakistan, and the American-Vietnam war. And almost nothing about any other parts of history.

I will say these are decent choices, given their relevance to current day events. And for painting a detailed picture of Britain's brutal colonial history and penchant for drawing straight lines on maps to divide up complex tribal groups and kingdoms that end in bloodshed. But I still could have used a cursory summary of some other sections.

[The Crusades.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades) [The Tudors.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Tudor) [The Stuarts.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Stuart) [The Russian Revolution.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution) [The Vikings.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikings) [Imperial China.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Empire) [The Industrial Revolution.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) [The Troubles.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles) World War II events other than the Holocaust. I know only the most sweeping gestures of what happened here, having filled in the blanks with the odd podcast and skimming Wikipedia articles.

But World War I feels like the most egregious oversight.

Until a few months ago, I couldn't have told you who was fighting whom, or how long it went on for. To defend myself a little, I did not grow up in England, but instead bopping around various countries in South East Asia, where there were no red poppies or minutes of silence on [Remembrance Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembrance_Day).

But then I went to see the play [War Horse](https://www.nationaltheatre.org.uk/productions/war-horse/) at the National Theatre in London<Footnote idName={1}>If you are anywhere near London I cannot recommend this strongly enough. The horse puppetry alone is gorgeous.</Footnote>. Originally a [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Horse_(novel)), about a young British boy and his horse, both sent off to endure trench warfare, shelling, gas, and no man's land, across France and Germany.

After I emotionally recovered from sobbing my way through the ending, I realised I had a lot of questions about the plain facts of the war. Why were the British involved? How many died? Who had the tanks? Why didn't the British have machine guns?

Trying to fill the gaping hole, I went looking for biographies and historical novels that would give me both broad overviews of the war and accurate personal accounts of what it was like to be in the trenches. Forgive me for being parochial, but being British, I gravitated towards stories from my country. I first tried reading Robert Graves' biography [Goodbye to All That](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good-Bye_to_All_That), widely considered a canonical account, but it mostly taught me that rampant homosexual sex among British public school boys isn't just a long-standing cultural joke. I didn't get many of the broad or specific details of the war I was looking for.

</GridColumns>

But then on a recommendation from Claude, I found [In Memoriam](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/705549/in-memoriam-by-alice-winn/) by Alice Winn, which was the exact book I wanted. Historical fiction, but tightly based on a broad range of first-hand accounts. Fiction can often tell a more comprehensive and representative story of history than any single "true" account by stitching together many people's lived experiences, then zooming out to explain the context across multiple characters.

The book is lightly romance, following two boys from public school to the front as officers, navigating illegal love. But it's more heavily horror, detailing the gory and brutal conditions of British soldiers living in deep mud, frequently sacrificed as machine gun fodder. There are vivid images that stick with you; limbs hanging out of the sides of trenches as the sodden mud around them fails to hold the walls in place, revealing bits of bodies buried in previous battles. Soldiers hiding under stacks of decomposing men for days in no man's land. Waves after wave men ordered over the top of trenches into walls of bullets in the [Somme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Somme). Captains point-blank shooting their own men, too terrified to move, to discourage cowardice. The wounded biting deep into their own arms and hands to stop from crying out, lest they give away their position or let others die attempting to save them. These kinds of details are not invented.

Listening to this story filled with pain and cruelty, while standing in my baking London house, in the middle of a brutal heatwave caused by a changing climate that will inflict catastrophic losses on the entire world, I considered how quickly we forget this level of suffering is possible. How easily it can become acceptable. In the way military leaders accepted that milions of men simply had to go over the top to their inhumane deaths.

I have only ever lived in times and places of peace and prosperity. The trauma of my great-grandparents who lived in those trenches feels very foreign to me. Me sitting here, MacBook Pro in hand, Aeron chair under my butt, snacking on chocolate-dipped almonds, posting about how we need better interfaces for agentic coding on Twitter. I am the furthest person from this kind of suffering.

But this book, this beautiful piece of fiction, did its job. It made the atrocities thinkable. It reminded me we are not as far as we like to imagine from mass, global, industrial-scale death. Just over a hundred years past it. Hopefully at least a few decades from it. Climate change and AI risk both loom ominously in my mind in these moments.

Anyway, if you're looking to feel something, [In Memoriam](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/705549/in-memoriam-by-alice-winn/) is a good starting point.
