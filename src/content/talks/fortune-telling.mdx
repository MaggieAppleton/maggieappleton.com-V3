<IntroParagraph>I recently got the chance to give a talk at [Granola](https://www.granola.ai/)'s [Design Engineering night](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL4tqAmtnUMjO4EmmwJ-D2edgLardYJrvv) about speculative design and practical tools for developing more interesting theories of the future. I think design engineers, in particular, are the perfect people to imagine and prototype novel software ideas.</IntroParagraph>

At [GitHub Next](https://githubnext.com), our team's job is to explore possible futures for software development by building prototypes. I share a bit about how we do that, followed by some practical techniques: collecting weak signals, building theories about where things are heading, and making artefacts that make those theories feel real and tangible.

<div data-slide="24" data-source-slide="html/17-right-and-early.html" data-fragment="-1">

So before I give you lots of advice on how to predict the future, I should prove I know something about this. There have been a few times that I've made early predictions about the way things were headed and been right about them.

</div>

<div data-slide="25" data-source-slide="html/18-right-and-early-example-1.html" data-fragment="-1">

First, in early 2020, in the depths of the COVID lockdown, I wrote a long essay on a concept called [digital gardening](/garden-history). It wasn't particularly well known at the time, but the theory was that the way we publish personal knowledge on the web was changing. It was beginning to move away from blogging, where you published perfect, finished pieces in reverse chronological order, and toward a more iterative, organic, and non-linear way of publishing. Like a garden you tend over time.

Digital gardening went on to become a bit of a phenomenon. It had a real peak in popularity around 2021-2022.

</div>

<div data-slide="26" data-source-slide="html/19-right-and-early-example-1-copy.html" data-fragment="-1">

As part of researching this idea, I built my own digital garden, which is the website you're reading right now. I've published well over 160 pieces and I'm still updating it all the time. It's a growing, living record of my thoughts.

</div>

<div data-slide="27" data-source-slide="html/20-right-and-early-example-2.html" data-fragment="-1">

I developed my next pet theory developed between late 2022 and early 2023. This was back in the days of GPT-3. ChatGPT had only just come out. I was working at [Elicit](https://elicit.com), a startup that uses language models to speed up the scientific literature review process.

There weren't many people using language models at the time, but I started to look ahead to what I thought was going to happen to content on the web once they did. And I correctly predicted the open web would be drowned in what, at the time, I called “AI-generated crap” because we didn't have the word “AI slop” yet.

So I wrote this talk called [The Expanding Dark Forest and Generative AI](/forest-talk). When I first gave the talk, I didn't really have much evidence of this happening. I only had some very mild indicators to point to. I continued to give this talk multiple times over the next two years, and by the final version, I had _way_ too much evidence. Just piles of horrifying Facebook posts to point to.

</div>

<div data-slide="28" data-source-slide="html/21-right-and-early-example-3.html" data-fragment="-1">

I developed my next theory in early 2024, which led to my talk "[Home Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers](/home-cooked-software)" presented at the Local-First conference in May.

At the time, people were still quite sceptical that language models would ever be able to write production-quality code. This was pre-agents and pre-Claude Code. We were still in the days of using tab to autocomplete in IDEs.

I predicted that language models would soon become as good as professional, human software developers, which would lead to an explosion in personal software, as well as the rise of "barefoot developers" semi-professional technical builders for local neighbourhoods and communities. I think it's quite easy to argue this has now come to pass.

Even at the time, a lot of people argued with me about the feasibility of language models writing working software end-to-end. I think most holdouts with that opinion finally caved in late 2025 with the release of Opus-4.5 and GPT-5.2, a year and a half after I gave this talk.

</div>

<div data-slide="31" data-source-slide="html/23-section-how-github-next-does-it.html" data-fragment="-1">

</div>

<div data-slide="32" data-source-slide="html/24-the-dream-of-labs-work.html" data-fragment="-1">

I think there's a stereotype that being on a labs team means you get to play around making prototypes all day, without having to worry about shipping to production.

</div>

<div data-slide="33" data-source-slide="html/25-kind-of-but-not-entirely.html" data-fragment="-1">

Sadly, that's not really the case.

</div>

<div data-slide="34" data-source-slide="html/26-lots-of-prototypes.html" data-fragment="-1">

We do get to make _lots and lots_ of prototypes...

</div>

<div data-slide="35" data-source-slide="html/27-nextcloud.html" data-fragment="-1">

In fact, we have an internal platform just for our team called Next Cloud where we all post our prototypes throughout the day. Everything goes on here, no matter how rough, small, silly, or early it is. Experiments and side discoveries go up alongside regular updates to our big, headline projects.

If you work on any kind of team trying to solve novel problems, having visiblity into what other people are exploring and playing with is essential. As well as having ways to react to, comment on, and riff off each other's ideas.

Getting this kind of quick feedback and working in the open works really well for us. I think it helps us calibrate and align our theories of the future.

</div>

<div data-slide="36" data-source-slide="html/28-nextcloud-copy.html" data-fragment="-1">

Just to show you some examples of the sort of things we put up: here is a demo from my coworker [Alex Gorischek](https://alexgorischek.com/) exploring a system that enforces a maintainer's guidelines for creating GitHub issues.

This is powered by a System One decision model like [Jev](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), which instantly judges whether you’ve met all the stated requirements before you can post the issue.

</div>

<div data-slide="37" data-source-slide="html/29-nextcloud-copy-2.html" data-fragment="-1">

Here's another from my coworker [Luke Edwards](https://lukeed.com/) exploring different primitives as first-class citizens on GitHub. Here he's playing with a real-time multiplayer canvas within a repo for drawing ideas with coworkers before turning them into agent work.

So those two examples are quite small and not-very-serious explorations of what _could_ exist on GitHub. But making tangible objects like these to look at is the first step in figuring out if these directions are interesting or useful to us.

</div>

<div data-slide="38" data-source-slide="html/30-figjam-ideas.html" data-fragment="-1">

We also regularly do group theory building inside a giant FigJam board. We recently all braindumped stickies in here on themes, specific ideas, and directions we think software and agentic development is headed. These could be anything from trends we're seeing to something we want to figure out to unanswered questions.

We then grouped these into buckets: new interactions and visual mediums, conceptual shifts and new nouns, new agent capabilities, types of validation, etc.

Doing this helps us figure out what everyone is seeing, what they're excited about, and what problems we can team up on.

</div>

<div data-slide="39" data-source-slide="html/31-but-we-also-need-to.html" data-fragment="-1">

But (obviously) we need to do a lot more than come up with plausible theories and make cool prototypes all day.

We need to prove our theories are ~likely to be correct with evidence.  
We need to have good intuition and good quality data. 
We need to play politics and convince other people our ideas are worth listening to.

And we _do_ need to have a credible exit to production. This is not a dreams lab, and this team in particular has a very good track record of shipping their wild bets to GitHub proper. For example: [GitHub Copilot](), [GitHub Spark](), [GitHub Workspaces](), and [Agentic Workflows]().

</div>

<div data-slide="40" data-source-slide="html/32-the-actual-job.html" data-fragment="-1">

The job involves looking around at the wider software engineering industry, noticing trends and patterns.

Then extrapolating from those signals to predict where things are headed.

Building compelling prototypes that explore what those predictions look like in practice and on screens. As well as showing what the right strategic play for GitHub in particular would be.

And lastly convincing our leaders and wider organisation that these ideas are worth investing in. That means dedicating time, people, and money to them when they seem especially promising.

</div>

<div data-slide="41" data-source-slide="html/33-the-actual-job-is-actually-hard.html" data-fragment="-1">

And that actual job is actually really hard. It is simple to describe, but both intellectually challenging and strategically complex to really do it well.

</div>

<div data-slide="42" data-source-slide="html/34-the-politics.html" data-fragment="-1">

And the political struggles are very real. Trying to make a large organisation made up of thousands of people, with many hundreds of millions of users, change course is challenging. This isn't because of any nefarious actors or specific dysfunction.

It's because any truly ambitious idea that changes the existing plan would mean disrupting existing roadmaps, convincing people to do a 180 on everything they care about, and will inevitably threathen some egos. “Not invented here” syndrome can happen anywhere.

</div>

<div data-slide="43" data-source-slide="html/35-the-politics-copy.html" data-fragment="-1">

But that is a whole different talk.

<div data-slide="44" data-source-slide="html/36-section-the-toolkit.html" data-fragment="-1">

</div>

<div data-slide="45" data-source-slide="html/37-the-toolkit.html" data-fragment="-1">

We're going to talk through six tools today: the futures cone, weak signals, second and third order effects, playing with ideas, future artefacts, and making your theories concrete and public.

</div>

<div data-slide="46" data-source-slide="html/38-futures-cone.html" data-fragment="-1">

I feel obliged to start with the futures cone because it's a hallmark of the field of futures studies and speculative design. Many of today’s tools come from that world.

The idea is simple. We sit on the “now” mark and look forward into the cone. The wider the angle of the cone, the more uncertain we are about the futures within in. Probable future scenarios 

Whenever you develop a new idea about the future, it's useful to ask yourself: which ring does it live in? The possible, plausible, probable or preferable?
